FM Transmitter Power: It Is Not Just Watts

Choosing an FM transmitter starts with one question: how far do you need to broadcast? Power, antenna height and terrain all affect coverage — but power is where you start.

Understanding Power Classes

Low Power: 0.5W – 100W

Best for: drive-in churches, small campuses, parking lots, indoor broadcasting, hobby stations.

PowerTypical range
0.5W (FU-05B)~500 m
7W (FU-7C)~2 km
15W (FU-15A)~3 km
25W (FU-25A)~5 km
50W (FMT5.0-50H)~8 km
100W (FSN-100B)~12 km

*Ranges assume a good antenna at 30–50 m height. Open terrain extends them; city terrain reduces them.*

Medium Power: 100W – 1KW

Best for: community radio, local stations, university radio.

Typical coverage: 10–40 km, depending on antenna height and terrain.

High Power: 1KW – 10KW+

Best for: regional and national broadcasters.

PowerTypical range
1KW30–50 km

5KW70–100 km
10KW100–150 km

Key Specifications to Compare

When comparing transmitters, do not just look at watts:

  • Exciter quality — determines audio fidelity and stability
  • Harmonic suppression — needed to pass licensing
  • Frequency stability — ±500 Hz or better
  • RDS/RBDS support — for station name and text on radios
  • Remote control — SNMP, web interface for monitoring
  • Power efficiency — solid-state designs beat tube designs
  • Redundancy — dual power supplies for broadcast-critical use

Licensing Matters

Transmitter power is tightly regulated in most countries:

  • Low power (<100W): often exempt or simplified licensing
  • Medium power (100W–1KW): usually requires a license
  • High power (1KW+): full broadcast license with frequency coordination

*Always check your local regulator before purchasing high-power equipment.*

Solid-State vs Tube Transmitters

Solid-state (modern standard):

  • ✅ Higher efficiency (60–75%)
  • ✅ Longer lifespan, lower maintenance
  • ✅ Safe to run without constant supervision
  • ✅ Compact rack-mount designs

Tube (legacy):

  • ❌ Lower efficiency (40–50%)
  • ❌ Regular tube replacement costs
  • ❌ Higher operating cost

Choose solid-state for new installations — it pays back quickly in power and maintenance savings.

Final Advice

1. Start smaller than you think — coverage surprises most first-time buyers

2. Invest in the antenna — it is as important as the transmitter

3. Budget for installation — feeder cable, tower, grounding

4. Buy from a supplier with local support — transmitters need service

Bottom line: match the transmitter to your licensing, budget and realistic coverage goal. A well-installed 50W transmitter with a good antenna often beats a badly installed 500W unit.
